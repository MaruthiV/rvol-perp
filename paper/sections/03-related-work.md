# 3. Related work

The variance perpetual described in this paper sits at the intersection of six literatures: (i) variance swap theory and static replication, (ii) realized variance econometrics, (iii) the variance risk premium, (iv) stochastic volatility models, (v) market microstructure and price-impact theory, and (vi) on-chain derivatives and DeFi oracle design. We situate our contribution within each thread below. The fuller annotated bibliography and per-entry "our use" commentary is deferred to `paper/LITERATURE.md` in the supplementary materials.

## 3.1 Variance swaps and static replication

The mathematical foundation for trading variance as a linear payoff rests on a sequence of results that begins with Neuberger's (1994) log contract — the observation that a payoff of the form $2\log(S_T/S_0)$ has a delta which, when dynamically hedged, locks in exposure to realized variance independent of the volatility path. Carr and Madan (1998) extended this into the framework of static replication via a strike-weighted strip of European options, and Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal, and Zou (1999) delivered the canonical practitioner derivation that every variance-swap dealer has used since. Britten-Jones and Neuberger (2000) formalized model-free implied variance from option prices, giving rise to the modern VIX methodology. Carr and Lee (2003, 2009) extended the replication argument to "robust" (model-free) settings and made precise the sense in which variance admits a static hedge while volatility does not — the latter introducing convexity adjustments that must be dynamically hedged. This result is the reason our contract is a variance perp rather than a volatility perp: a $\sqrt{\mathrm{RV}}$ payoff would require arbitrageurs to dynamically hedge an on-chain convexity term, which is incompatible with our O(1) oracle-free construction (§11).

**Our specific debt.** The single most important citation in this thread is Demeterfi et al. (1999), not for the replication derivation itself but for the **capped variance swap convention** — the industry-standard practice of clipping the short-side payoff at $c\cdot K_{\text{var}}$ with $c$ typically 2.5. Every OTC variance swap traded on a dealer balance sheet since that paper has been capped, and for a reason we empirically rediscovered in §10: the uncapped short side has unbounded loss against a statistic (variance) that is bounded below by zero and unbounded above. Our Phase 8.6 design adopts the same 2.5× cap, applied per position at each position's own entry strike, making this paper — to our knowledge — the first on-chain implementation of the Demeterfi cap in a DeFi perpetual.

## 3.2 Realized variance econometrics

The estimator we use — a sum of squared high-frequency log returns over a fixed window — is an instance of the realized variance estimator studied by Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold, and Labys (2001, 2003), whose foundational result is that $\mathrm{RV} = \sum r^2$ converges to the integrated variance of the underlying process as sampling frequency increases. Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (2002) provided the distributional theory and asymptotic normality of log-$\mathrm{RV}$. Subsequent work addressed noise-robustness: Zhang, Mykland, and Aït-Sahalia (2005) via multi-scale subsampling and Barndorff-Nielsen, Hansen, Lunde, and Shephard (2008) via realized kernels, both of which correct the bias introduced by microstructure noise at tick frequencies. Corsi (2009) introduced the HAR-RV model — a parsimonious linear regression of realized variance on its daily, weekly, and monthly lags — which is the standard forecasting benchmark in the realized-variance literature.

**Our specific position.** Our estimator is a minute-frequency ABDL-style realized variance with a MAD-based outlier cap. We deliberately do not use realized kernels or multi-scale estimators, for two reasons. First, the O(1) per-update requirement of the on-chain oracle rules out any estimator whose bandwidth or subsample structure changes the accounting each minute. Second, at 1-minute sampling microstructure noise is already small relative to the underlying return variance, and the 4σ MAD cap acts as an outlier filter that handles the most damaging noise-like contributions directly. The MAD cap in an adversarial-manipulation-resistance role is, to our knowledge, not previously studied in the realized-variance literature — prior robust estimators were designed against honest noise, not adversarial manipulation, and the distinction matters for the parameter choices we make in §4.2 and the cost analysis of §8. We do not forecast $\mathrm{RV}$; the paper is concerned with spot-pricing it as a settlement index, and HAR-RV is cited as the forecasting benchmark we would use in a follow-up.

## 3.3 Variance risk premium

The economic case for the contract rests on the positivity of the variance risk premium (VRP) — the expected spread between model-free implied variance and subsequently realized variance. This premium has been documented extensively for equity indices: Bakshi and Kapadia (2003) provided the first clean identification via delta-hedged gains on S&P 500 options, Carr and Wu (2009) gave the canonical cross-sectional study and established the synthetic-variance-swap measurement methodology that we follow in §6.5, and Bollerslev, Tauchen, and Zhou (2009) showed that VRP predicts future stock returns — establishing it as a priced risk factor rather than an option-pricing artifact. Todorov (2010) decomposed VRP into diffusive and jump components and showed that jumps dominate, a result that has implications for our Phase 5/8.5 methodology which used a pure-diffusion stochastic vol Monte Carlo (§10.1).

**The closest comparable.** Alexander and Imeraj (2021) measured the variance risk premium of BTC using Deribit DVOL and a model-free implied variance construction. They find the BTC VRP is positive, persistent, and substantially larger than developed-market equity VRP — consistent with the intuition that the marginal buyer of crypto tail insurance is willing to pay a larger premium than in mature markets. Our §6.5 measurement of $\mathrm{VRP} = (\mathrm{DVOL}/100)^2/365 - \mathrm{RV30}_{\text{forward}}$ on our own sample is a confirmatory replication of their result, using a slightly different sample period and a different realized-variance estimator. We report a mean VRP of $+14$ annualized vol points with a 71% positive-day fraction, consistent with their range. Alexander and Imeraj is the reference a reviewer will most naturally ask us to compare against, and we do.

## 3.4 Stochastic volatility models

The Phase 5 lifecycle simulator uses a log-normal stochastic volatility process in the Hull and White (1987) family, calibrated to minute-level BTC returns. The alternative square-root variance model of Heston (1993) is the industry standard for equity options; the log-normal choice was made because BTC log-volatility is more symmetric under empirical fit than the square-root specification. Bates (1996) and Andersen, Benzoni, and Lund (2002) added Poisson jumps to the diffusion and demonstrated empirically that jumps are essential for capturing tail behavior on equity index returns; Christoffersen, Jacobs, and Mimouni (2010) reached a similar conclusion via a broader model comparison.

**The methodological lesson.** Our Phase 5 calibration to a pure-diffusion log-normal SV model produced a simulated 60-day tier-1 liquidation rate of 0.0% at 10× leverage — which, when cross-checked against historical replay in Phase 8.5, turned out to be an 80% real liquidation rate (§10.3). The gap between the two numbers is the exact phenomenon that Bates (1996) and Andersen-Benzoni-Lund (2002) warned about in the equity case: a diffusive SV model without jumps systematically underestimates tail behavior, and a margin calibration that trusts the model will be systematically optimistic in precisely the regime where the contract needs to survive. We cite these papers as the specific prior warning that we did not heed sufficiently at calibration time — our recommendation in §13 that any future oracle-free derivative on an unbounded statistic be calibrated by historical replay (not by diffusion MC alone) is a direct operationalization of the Bates/Andersen-Benzoni-Lund lesson.

## 3.5 Market microstructure and manipulation cost

Our Phase 7 manipulation-cost analysis uses the Kyle (1985) linear price impact model: the price change induced by a marketable order is proportional to the order size, with the proportionality constant $\lambda$ determined by order-book depth. Almgren and Chriss (2000) extended the framework to decompose price impact into temporary and permanent components and derived optimal execution trajectories; Huberman and Stanzl (2004) showed that no-arbitrage constraints require the permanent component to be linear in order size, theoretically justifying the use of Kyle's linear form in large-scale manipulation analyses. Obizhaeva and Wang (2013) studied limit-order-book resilience and showed that temporary impact decays as depth refills.

**Our specific use.** We calibrate $\lambda$ directly from Hyperliquid's BTC-perp order book (approximately \$5M per 1% price move) and apply the Kyle model to adversarial attack geometries in §8. The choice of Kyle over more sophisticated models is deliberate and conservative: non-linear impact functions concave in quantity would make large moves more expensive per dollar, and resilient-book models would make sustained attacks more expensive because depth recovers only partially between adversarial minutes. Our cost bounds are therefore lower bounds on the true adversarial cost. We are not aware of prior work applying Kyle-style impact analysis to a **variance oracle** rather than to a spot-price oracle; the analysis is methodologically standard but the application to the manipulation-resistance of a realized-variance index is novel.

## 3.6 On-chain derivatives and oracle-free design

The proximate antecedents of our work are the on-chain derivatives venues and the emerging literature on oracle-free protocol design. **Panoptic** [Lambert et al., 2023] and **InfinityPools** established the principle that an on-chain derivative can be settled from the venue's own LP-position state rather than from an external price feed — the first "oracle-free" derivatives in the strict sense. Panoptic constructs perpetual American options by borrowing and deploying Uniswap v3 LP positions; the payoff is a deterministic function of the LP position's token balances, which are themselves a function of the pool's trading history. This is the structural inspiration for our work: we extend the principle from spot-dependent products (options on current price) to path-dependent products (variance over a 30-day window), which requires maintaining bounded O(1) state evolving deterministically per observation.

**The closest on-chain cousin** is **Squeeth** (Opyn / Paradigm, 2021), a perpetual on ETH-squared that provides convexity exposure and is mechanically the most similar existing product to ours. Squeeth and our variance perp are both perpetuals on non-linear functions of on-venue prices; they differ in four important respects. First, Squeeth's index is $p^2$ — a function of current spot, not of the price path — while ours is $\Sigma r^2$ over a window. Second, Squeeth requires Uniswap v3 as a hedging venue and inherits AMM oracle risk from the pool mechanics; ours reads directly from the host venue's own trade stream. Third, Squeeth is not directly linear in realized variance; it is a proxy for variance exposure via $p^2$ that matches true variance only under specific stochastic-vol assumptions. Fourth, and most importantly for the contribution of this paper, Squeeth does not face the unbounded-short-side problem that §10 documents for variance — because $p^2$ is bounded by spot price action, not by the cumulative variance of a 30-day path — and therefore does not need a Demeterfi-style payoff cap. We cite Squeeth as prior art and differentiate carefully; Squeeth remains the correct comparison for any reader approaching this paper from the perspective of existing on-chain vol products.

**The funding mechanism.** Angeris, Chitra, Evans, and Lorig (2023, "A primer on perpetuals") provide a formal treatment of perpetual contract pricing and funding-rate convergence under various funding designs. Our clamped-linear funding mechanism (§7) is a bounded variant of the stateless proportional family analyzed in their paper, with parameter choices justified empirically rather than derived from a closed-form no-arbitrage argument. We cite Angeris et al. as the methodological foundation for the funding analysis.

**Oracle manipulation as a DeFi-native threat model.** Eskandari et al. (2024) provide a systematic survey of DeFi oracle architectures and documented manipulation attacks (bZx, Harvest, Mango, etc.); Qin, Zhou, Livshits, and Gervais (2021) quantify flash-loan-based oracle attacks and attacker profits in practice; the Bank for International Settlements (2023, Bulletin No. 76) establishes that oracle risk is institutionally recognized at the policy level. These papers collectively motivate the "oracle-free matters" framing of our introduction (§1) — oracle manipulation is not a theoretical concern but a first-class, empirically observed threat class in DeFi, and any protocol that can eliminate the oracle dependency should.

**Existing crypto volatility products.** Deribit DVOL and Volmex BVIV are 30-day implied volatility indices computed from options books using VIX-style model-free methodologies [CBOE 2003; Britten-Jones and Neuberger 2000]. Both are centralized (computed off-chain by the respective venues) and forward-looking (implied, not realized). On-chain options venues — Lyra, Premia, Dopex — give vega exposure through option Greeks but with nonlinear payoff, path dependence, and expiry friction; none offer a linear, perpetual, realized-variance exposure. Hyperliquid's HIP-3 permissionless listing protocol (2025) is our deployment target and provides the infrastructure under which the contract can be listed without Hyperliquid governance approval, subject to the 500k HYPE staking requirement.

## 3.7 Summary: what we inherit, what we extend

The paper inherits the linear-variance-payoff rationale (Carr-Lee), the 2.5× cap convention (Demeterfi et al.), the realized-variance estimator (ABDL), the VRP measurement methodology (Carr-Wu, Alexander-Imeraj for BTC), the Kyle price-impact model (Kyle, Almgren-Chriss), the oracle-free architectural principle (Panoptic, InfinityPools), and the clamped-linear funding family (Angeris et al.). None of these components is novel individually. The novelty — as we argue in §1 and revisit in §13 — is in the synthesis: the first oracle-free perpetual on a **path-dependent** function (not spot), the first on-chain implementation of the capped-variance-swap convention, the first empirically-calibrated margin system for a crypto variance instrument against real regime-break history, and the honest-negative-result methodology of §10 that turned a failed Monte Carlo calibration into a publishable finding. The remainder of the paper is the execution of that synthesis.
