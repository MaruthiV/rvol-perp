# 2. Introduction

Crypto markets have deep spot and perpetual-futures liquidity on dozens of venues, a mature on-chain options ecosystem across several Layer-1 and Layer-2 chains, and real-time implied-volatility indices published by centralized venues (Deribit DVOL, Volmex BVIV). Despite all of this, no on-chain venue today offers a **linear, perpetual, oracle-free** exposure to **realized** volatility — the quantity that vol-sellers, miners, dealers, and tail-risk funds actually need to trade against. This paper builds one, calibrates it against six years of real BTC history, and documents an empirical stress test that turned a conventional design into an honest one.

## 2.1 The gap

A vol-selling desk today has three imperfect ways to express a short-vol view in crypto. It can (a) write options on Deribit or an on-chain options venue and take on path-dependent, nonlinear, expiry-constrained risk that requires dynamic hedging of its Greeks. It can (b) short a centralized implied-vol future on DVOL, inheriting all of the counterparty and oracle dependencies of a centralized venue. Or it can (c) run synthetic variance-swap replication across a strip of options — the Carr-Lee (2003) construction — which in principle gives linear variance exposure but in practice requires active rebalancing across dozens of strikes, is prohibitively expensive in gas and slippage on-chain, and has never been implemented at production scale in DeFi.

A miner or tail-risk fund on the long side of vol has the mirror-image problem. The fund wants to pay a predictable premium in exchange for a payoff that scales linearly with realized variance during a market dislocation — the textbook insurance trade — and has no venue offering exactly that contract. Buying options works but introduces theta, gamma, and strike selection. Buying DVOL futures works but settles at a centralized expiry against a feed the fund cannot verify.

The gap we identify is the **product** that would close this structural mismatch: a perpetual futures contract whose settlement index is the rolling realized variance of a major crypto asset, computed in a way that any trader or counterparty can verify from on-chain state alone, with a margin and funding system calibrated to make it safe to leverage at some honest amount. "Oracle-free" is not an aesthetic preference in this setting — it is the property that lets the contract scale: if the oracle is the chain itself, the contract inherits the chain's trust model and adds no new trust root. If the oracle is an external feed, the contract inherits that feed's manipulation surface, and history has shown that manipulation surface to be the single largest source of realized losses in DeFi to date [Qin et al. 2021; Eskandari et al. 2024].

## 2.2 Why this is non-trivial

The reason no such product exists today is that constructing one is hard in four distinct ways, and the difficulties compound.

**(i) Path-dependence.** Spot-dependent derivatives (vanilla options, Squeeth's $p^2$ perpetual) can be settled from a single current price read. Variance is path-dependent — it depends on the sum of squared returns over a 30-day window — which forces the contract to maintain bounded, deterministic, O(1)-updatable state over the full window. The Panoptic and InfinityPools architectures, which pioneered oracle-free settlement for on-chain derivatives, only address spot-dependent payoffs; the extension to path-dependent statistics is not automatic.

**(ii) Manipulation resistance.** If the settlement index is computed from on-venue trade flow, any trader with capital can attempt to print off-market returns into the index by trading the underlying. The defense — a robust outlier cap on per-minute return contributions — must be designed such that the cap is inactive on real moves and active on adversarial moves, and must be verifiable on-chain at O(1) per update. The naive choice of a sample-standard-deviation-based cap actively defeats itself because large adversarial moves inflate the sample standard deviation and raise the cap on their own contribution.

**(iii) Fundamental asymmetry of variance.** Variance is bounded below by zero and unbounded above. A short variance position therefore has unbounded downside against a realistic path. This is not a calibration bug — it is a property of the variance operator — and it is the reason every OTC variance swap traded since 1998 has been a **capped** variance swap [Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal, Zou 1999]. Our Phase 8.5 historical replay (§10) empirically rediscovers this fact on the BTC 2020–2026 sample: at 10× leverage with an uncapped payoff, 80% of historical 60-day windows produce liquidation. The fix — a per-position 2.5× payoff cap — is the TradFi canonical solution, and this paper is its first on-chain implementation.

**(iv) Honest margin calibration.** Our first-attempt calibration used a log-normal stochastic-volatility Monte Carlo fitted to minute-level BTC returns, reported 0% liquidation at 10× leverage across all tiers, and was flatly contradicted by historical replay. The reason is the same one Bates (1996) and Andersen, Benzoni, and Lund (2002) documented for equity indices: diffusive stochastic-vol models without jumps systematically underestimate tail behavior, and a margin calibration that trusts the model will be catastrophically optimistic in exactly the regime where the contract needs to survive. Fixing the calibration requires replacing Monte Carlo with historical replay as the calibration gate — which is an operational choice the field has generally not made.

These four difficulties are why the gap has persisted.

## 2.3 Contributions

This paper makes four contributions, each corresponding to one of the difficulties above.

**C1: The first oracle-free perpetual on a path-dependent function.** We specify, implement, and calibrate a perpetual futures contract whose settlement index is the rolling 30-calendar-day realized variance of BTC 1-minute log returns, computed entirely from the host venue's own trade history with no external feed. The on-chain oracle maintains a 43,200-entry ring buffer of capped squared returns plus a 1,440-entry MAD buffer, for a total state footprint of approximately 350 kB, and updates in $O(1)$ amortized time per minute. The contract is deployable under Hyperliquid's HIP-3 permissionless-listing protocol.

**C2: A manipulation-cost analysis with a verifiable lower bound.** We apply a Kyle (1985) linear price-impact model calibrated to Hyperliquid's BTC-perp order book depth to a suite of attack geometries against the real 3.28M-minute BTC return history. The 4σ MAD cap reduces the contribution of any single-minute spike with $k \ge 15\sigma$ by **at least 93%**, and the minimum cost to move the annualized index by one volatility point across every tested attack geometry is **\$3.7M**. A 5-vol-point move costs more than \$20M end-to-end. These bounds are lower bounds on adversarial cost under a conservative linear impact model; a more realistic book would make the adversary's cost strictly higher.

**C3: An honest empirical margin calibration with a publishable negative result.** We document the full sequence of our margin calibration: (1) a Monte Carlo calibration that reported 0% liquidation at 10× leverage and was wrong; (2) a historical replay against real BTC history that showed 80% liquidation at the same leverage; (3) the identification of the unbounded-variance asymmetry as the fundamental cause; (4) the adoption of the Demeterfi et al. (1999) capped-variance-swap convention with $c = 2.5$; and (5) the recalibration against the capped historical distribution, which yields a tier table with a maximum leverage of **1.5×** at tier 1 and both long and short empirical liquidation rates below the 5% gate at every tier. The collapse of the leverage ceiling from the Phase 8 design claim of 10× to the honest Phase 8.6 number of 1.5× is the single most important quantitative result in the paper, and the Phase 8.5 replay failure is the paper's methodological anchor.

**C4: A product-family framework.** We generalize the construction to an explicit four-condition template (on-chain computability, manipulation-resistant estimator, economically meaningful capped payoff, natural counterparty flow) that extends to any on-chain-computable statistic satisfying the conditions. We sketch four concrete instantiations — a gas-volatility perpetual, a BTC-ETH realized-correlation perpetual, a stablecoin depeg-risk perpetual, and a self-referential pre-IPO-style instrument — showing that the oracle-free path-dependent architecture is not specific to variance but applies across qualitatively different statistics and asset classes. The variance perp is the prototype; the rest of the product family is a direct application.

## 2.4 The anchor: what §10 finds and why it matters

The anchor of the paper is §10, "Historical stress test: a negative result and its canonical fix." We present it up-front because it reframes how the reader should approach the rest of the contract design.

The §10 sequence is: (a) a stochastic-vol Monte Carlo said 10× leverage is safe; (b) real BTC history said 80% of positions at 10× get liquidated; (c) the discrepancy traced to the unbounded-short-side asymmetry of variance; (d) the canonical fix is the 1999 Demeterfi 2.5× cap; (e) recalibrated against the capped historical distribution, the honest leverage ceiling is 1.5×, and both sides clear the liquidation gate at every tier.

Three things about this sequence are worth stating in the introduction. First, it is a **publishable negative result**: the Monte Carlo calibration failed, we say so, and the failure is the empirical motivation for the capped design. Most papers in this space report the final, successful calibration and bury the failed earlier attempts; we argue that the failed attempt is more valuable than the final calibration, because it is a transferable lesson about the right methodology for any future oracle-free derivative on an unbounded statistic.

Second, it is a **recapitulation of a 1999 result in a 2026 context**. Goldman Sachs's quantitative strategies group, writing in 1999 about OTC variance swaps on equity indices, reached the same conclusion we reached in 2026 about BTC variance perpetuals: the uncapped short side cannot be insured at a dealer, so it must be capped. We did not know this was an inevitability until we ran the replay; having run it, we are able to be precise about why the canonical 2.5× cap is correct for BTC and not merely convenient.

Third, the result **sets the honest expectation for the product**. The leverage ceiling of 1.5× is not a marketing-friendly number compared to the 10×-and-up leverages offered on spot perpetuals. We argue in §10.9 that this is the correct and honest number: variance is a high-volatility quantity, and genuine high leverage on it is unsafe regardless of the venue or oracle design. The paper's contribution in the margin analysis is to demonstrate that the honest answer is 1.5× rather than 10×, with the data and the methodology to back the claim.

## 2.5 Organization

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 3 situates the work in the existing literatures on variance swaps, realized variance econometrics, variance risk premium, stochastic vol models, market microstructure, and on-chain derivatives. Section 4 specifies the index: the MAD cap, the rolling 30-day window, the validity flag, and the invariants an implementation must satisfy. Section 5 documents the data sources and quality controls. Section 6 reports the empirical properties of the 30-day realized variance on BTC — persistence, regime behavior, DVOL comparison, and the variance risk premium. Section 7 specifies the funding mechanism and its empirical convergence properties. Section 8 presents the manipulation-cost analysis and the $3.7M-per-vol-point bound. Section 9 specifies the margin system, the capped payoff, and the tier table. Section 10 is the historical stress test and the negative-result narrative. Section 11 describes the on-chain realization and the deployment sequence. Section 12 generalizes the construction to a product family and sketches four worked applications. Section 13 discusses the limits of the calibration, regime dependence, and the interpretation of the 1.5× leverage ceiling. Section 14 concludes. Appendix A contains the formal index spec and invariants; Appendix B contains the full calibration tables.

All empirical numbers in the paper trace to a specific script in the accompanying repository under `scripts/`, and each analysis script is deterministic given the same input. A full reproduction of every figure and table in the paper is possible from a clean machine in a few hours of compute.
