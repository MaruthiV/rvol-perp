# 13. Discussion

This section addresses the limits of the paper's empirical claims, the interpretation of the honest 1.5× leverage ceiling, the assumptions that would have to change to change the calibration, and the operational concerns that any reader considering deployment should weigh. We also sketch the follow-up research agenda that the paper makes possible.

## 13.1 What the 2020–2026 sample can and cannot tell us

Every empirical claim in the paper — the manipulation-cost bounds of §8, the tier table of §9, the historical liquidation rates of §10 — rests on a single six-year sample of BTC price history from January 2020 to March 2026. The sample is unusually rich: it contains the COVID crash, the 2021 bull run and May-2021 liquidation cascade, the LUNA collapse, the FTX bankruptcy, the SVB banking stress episode, and the spot-ETF approval. Four of these are regime-break events of the kind that any honest margin calibration must survive; one (COVID) is a roughly once-in-a-decade tail event on the equity analogue (the 2008 financial crisis) and has no pre-2020 analogue in BTC's short history.

The sample is nevertheless a single draw from an unknown distribution of future regimes, and the Phase 8.6 tier table is fitted to the worst-case 7-day excursions observed in that draw. Three distinct concerns follow.

**The next regime may be qualitatively unlike any in the sample.** A sustained multi-month high-vol plateau at 150%+ annualized — not a single spike to 147.7% like COVID, but a *persistent* post-crisis regime at that level — is not in the training set. Neither is a regime of sustained sub-20% realized vol, which would test the MAD estimator at its degenerate lower end. We cannot extrapolate the tier table to regimes outside the convex hull of the sample, and the paper's claim is therefore conditional: **conditional on future regimes being qualitatively drawn from a distribution similar to 2020–2026, the Phase 8.6 tier table passes its gates**. The launch plan (§11.5) includes quarterly recalibration reviews using the expanding history and a discretionary recalibration trigger if observed live liquidation rates exceed 2× the historical-replay gate over any rolling 90-day window; this is the operational response to the out-of-sample concern, not a theoretical fix for it.

**Six years is one degree of freedom in the tail.** Even accepting that the sample is representative, 1,124 rolling 7-day windows with five or six regime breaks is a small sample for estimating the tail of a heavy-tailed distribution. The 95% and 99% quantiles of the worst-excursion distribution are estimated with wide confidence intervals, and the "both sides clear the 5% gate" statement has a non-trivial sampling error on the short-side 3.65% number at tier 1 in particular. A bootstrap over the sample would give confidence intervals on the gate pass; we have not run this analysis because the operational response (launch with 50% position caps for the first 14 days, expand to full caps only after live observation) provides a much stronger empirical validation than any resampling procedure could.

**The margin calibration is fitted, not falsified.** The tier table is the tightest IM schedule that passes the 5% gate on the 2020–2026 sample. It has no margin buffer built in above what the data demand. A more conservative choice — for instance, pushing every IM rate up by 10 percentage points — would pass the gate with headroom and would cost users only a proportional amount of capital efficiency. We did not make this choice because the paper is meant to report the honest calibration, not a padded one; but a production deployment may reasonably decide to launch with a padded table and tighten over time as the empirical base expands. The paper presents the thinnest calibration consistent with the data, and the deployer gets to choose how much additional buffer to add.

## 13.2 The interpretation of 1.5× leverage

The honest leverage ceiling of 1.5× at tier 1 is the paper's most counterintuitive quantitative result for readers coming from the spot-perp world, where 10× or 20× leverages are routine and the main constraint is the user's risk appetite, not the contract's margin math. Three reframings help place the 1.5× number in context.

**Variance is a high-volatility quantity.** A perpetual on BTC-USD spot has an underlying that moves by a few percent a day under normal conditions and ten percent or so on stress days. A perpetual on 30-day realized variance has an underlying that can move by 50% or more within a week during a regime transition, and by multiples during a crisis (§10.4's 10.8× variance move during COVID). The right reference class for the 1.5× number is not "BTC spot perpetual at 10× leverage" but "a hypothetical perpetual on the VIX at some realistic leverage," and VIX futures — which are the closest TradFi comparable — trade at effective margin rates of 30%–75% depending on tenor, corresponding to leverages of roughly 1.3×–3.3×. In that reference class, 1.5× is in the middle of the range and unexceptional.

**Leverage is not the product's selling point.** The economic case for the contract (§6.5) rests on the +14 annualized-vol-points variance risk premium. At 1.5× leverage on tier 1 with \$50k vega-notional, the expected annual carry on collateral is roughly 21% — comparable to any other structural carry strategy in crypto and attractive without needing higher leverage to make the numbers work. The product competes on **direct linear exposure to realized variance, oracle-free settlement, and no expiry**, not on leverage. A trader looking for 10× exposure to variance should use options, where the nonlinear payoff gives effective leverage through gamma rather than through margin.

**The cap is the source of the constraint, and the cap is the right design.** A higher leverage ceiling is achievable if we remove the 2.5× cap, but §10 showed that the uncapped design is not leverageable at any rate. A higher leverage ceiling is also achievable if we adopt a much larger cap (say 5×), but the short-side worst-case loss scales linearly with the cap increment, and the IM rates would have to rise proportionally — net effect near zero in leverage terms and worse in capital efficiency. The 2.5× cap is pinned by the TradFi OTC convention for reasons that turn out to be empirically sensible at BTC scale, and moving it in either direction makes the product worse. The leverage ceiling is therefore a derived quantity of the cap choice, and the cap choice is the load-bearing design decision.

## 13.3 What would change the calibration

Any of the following changes would require recomputing the tier table and potentially changing the cap:

**Hold period.** The paper calibrates against 7-day holds. A product targeting 30-day roll traders would need to use 30-day holds in the calibration, which pushes tier-1 short liquidations above the 5% gate at the current IM rates — the IM rates would have to rise. A product targeting intraday traders (1-day holds) could accommodate lower IM rates. We chose 7 days because it is the shortest operationally meaningful holding period for a vol seller working against a monthly VRP carry and because it is the longest hold at which the capped contract comfortably passes the 5% gate at all tiers. Section 9.6 discusses the hold-period assumption explicitly; any deployer changing the target user base should recalibrate.

**Funding-inclusive PnL.** The historical-replay calibration excludes funding payments, isolating the pure mark-to-market behavior of the contract under the worst-case index excursions. Including funding would help the short side (it collects the VRP carry continuously) and marginally hurt the long side, tilting the calibration slightly in favor of shorts. We chose to exclude funding from the calibration to report the conservative case — funding is a second-order effect on position solvency compared to the index move itself (§7.4.3), and including it would make the tier table look rosier without improving the fundamental solvency guarantee.

**Cross-position netting.** The paper treats each position independently. A portfolio margin system that nets opposing positions would lower effective margin requirements materially, since a dispersion desk running simultaneous long and short positions across different entry strikes has much less aggregate exposure than the sum of its per-position notionals. We do not implement portfolio margin in the reference contract because it adds complexity and because the product is targeted at single-position traders in the first instance; a follow-up analysis of portfolio-margin calibration is natural future work.

**Insurance fund sizing.** The \$500k seed and \$5M replenishment target (§9.5) are calibrated to the tier-1 worst-case residual under simultaneous worst-case liquidations. A deployer choosing to launch with larger tier caps or more tiers would need to resize the insurance fund accordingly; the sizing logic is straightforward but the specific numbers are calibrated to the Phase 8.6 table and do not transfer directly to other calibrations.

## 13.4 Governance and parameter tunability

We have designed the contract with a deliberately minimal set of tunable parameters and a deliberately tight freeze protocol. The index spec parameters ($\sigma_{\text{cap}} = 4$, window = 30 days, MAD lookback = 1,440 minutes, validity threshold = 90%) are frozen at launch and are not governance-modifiable. The funding parameters ($k = 100$, $c = 0.001$) are governance-modifiable but only with a 14-day advance notice. The margin tier table is governance-modifiable with the same advance notice. The payoff cap ($c = 2.5$) is frozen at launch and cannot be modified — any change to the cap would require a new contract deployment, because the cap determines the IM rates through calibration and changing it mid-flight would leave legacy positions under the old cap convention while new positions use the new one.

The reason for the minimal tunability is audit-friendliness: a contract whose parameters can be changed by vote is a contract whose effective behavior depends on the history of governance actions, which makes historical replays of "what would this contract have done" ambiguous. We prefer the opposite extreme: the contract behaves identically in every historical replay because none of its parameters can be retroactively altered. Section 4.6 notes the tradeoff — a static cap threshold is less responsive to regime change than a governable one — and we come down in favor of audit-friendliness for v1.

## 13.5 Residual risks we do not solve

Three risks remain, and we flag them explicitly because they are not addressable by better calibration or better contract design within the scope of this paper.

**Cross-venue correlation attack.** An adversary who cannot cheaply move the BTC price on Hyperliquid may still be able to move it on a cheaper venue (a smaller perp venue with thin depth) and rely on cross-venue arbitrage to propagate the move to HL. The §8 cost analysis bounds the cost of directly attacking HL; it does not bound the cost of attacking a thin venue and waiting for the shock to arrive. The mitigation is to monitor for anomalous price moves on HL relative to a basket of spot and perp venues, and to pause funding if a divergence exceeds a statistical threshold. This is a runtime monitoring problem, not a contract-design problem, and we do not implement it in the reference contract.

**Liquidity evaporation during a regime break.** The insurance fund is sized for simultaneous tier-1 liquidations at normal order-book depth. In a real regime break, depth on the underlying venue may fall by 10× or more, which would both reduce the margin-of-safety on liquidation fills and expose the fund to a larger residual per liquidation. The launch plan includes a circuit breaker that can freeze the contract during observable depth stress, but the contract's behavior during the several minutes between the start of a depth collapse and the trigger of the breaker is not fully modeled. A deployer may reasonably choose to widen the breaker's trigger threshold at the cost of occasional false positives.

**Regulatory status of on-chain variance perpetuals.** The paper takes no position on the regulatory classification of the contract in any jurisdiction. A variance perpetual is arguably a swap under US CFTC rules, a financial contract for differences under various European frameworks, and an unregulated derivative under most crypto-friendly offshore regimes. HIP-3 deployments are subject to the Hyperliquid Foundation's listing policy and to whatever regulatory regime applies to HL itself. The content of this paper is a technical specification and an empirical calibration; the decision to deploy is a separate question we do not address.

## 13.6 Follow-up research

The paper opens several specific research directions.

**Extension to ETH, SOL, and other deep-perp assets.** The index spec, funding mechanism, and margin calibration procedure are asset-agnostic in principle. The question of whether the Phase 8.6 tier table transfers to ETH or requires recalibration is a straightforward empirical exercise: rerun the historical-replay pipeline against the ETH perp history. We expect the calibration to be similar in shape (the same asymmetry argument applies) but different in level (ETH vol is higher than BTC vol on average and may require tighter IM rates). A parallel Phase 2–10 pass on ETH is the natural next empirical project.

**Jump-diffusion Monte Carlo for prospective stress testing.** The paper uses historical replay as the calibration gate because diffusive SV Monte Carlo was empirically insufficient (§10.1). A jump-diffusion model in the Bates (1996) or Andersen-Benzoni-Lund (2002) family, calibrated to both minute-level moments and 60-day tail excursions, might be capable of reproducing the COVID-2020 tail and would allow prospective stress testing of regimes that have not yet occurred. We did not pursue this direction because historical replay was sufficient for the v1 calibration, but it is the right direction for any calibration that wants to extrapolate beyond the sample.

**Formal verification of the on-chain contract.** The determinism invariant of §4.7 — that the Python reference and the Solidity contract produce bit-identical output on the same inputs — is currently verified by a unit-test harness. It can be promoted to a formal verification problem: prove that the fixed-point arithmetic in the Solidity implementation matches the IEEE-754 float arithmetic in the Python reference up to a specified tolerance on all inputs in a defined domain. This is within reach of current formal-methods tooling and would eliminate an entire class of launch-gate concerns.

**Empirical validation of the product-family framework.** Section 12 sketches four applications of the general construction (gas-vol, correlation, depeg, self-referential). Each is a paper of its own. The gas-vol perp is the most immediately addressable because Ethereum gas history is trivially available and the natural counterparty populations are well-defined; a calibration and deployment of that product would provide an independent validation of the framework on a qualitatively different statistic.

**Portfolio margin and cross-position netting.** As noted in §13.3, the per-position calibration is conservative relative to a portfolio-margin system. The theoretical and empirical analysis of portfolio margin on a variance perp — especially for dispersion traders running long and short positions at different entry strikes — is a natural follow-up.

## 13.7 What the paper actually delivers

Summarizing the paper against the three contributions of §1:

- **C1 (first oracle-free path-dependent perpetual):** delivered, fully specified, with a reference implementation and a deployable HIP-3 design.
- **C2 (manipulation-cost analysis with a verifiable lower bound):** delivered, with \$3.7M-per-vol-point as the headline bound and a cap reduction of ≥93% for $k \ge 15\sigma$.
- **C3 (honest empirical margin calibration with a negative-result methodology):** delivered, with the failed Monte Carlo calibration, the 80% replay-failure discovery, the Demeterfi-cap fix, and the Phase 8.6 recalibration documenting the honest 1.5× leverage ceiling.
- **C4 (product-family framework):** delivered as a design sketch with four worked examples and an explicit four-condition template; leaves the per-product calibration to follow-up work.

The paper does not deliver a live production contract; that is the deployment phase, which is the launch plan of §11.5 applied after publication. It does not deliver a jump-diffusion SV model calibrated to reproduce the COVID tail; that is future work. It does not deliver an ETH calibration; that is a parallel empirical exercise. And it does not deliver a portfolio-margin analysis; that is a direct follow-up. Each of these is flagged above as a specific research direction, and each is within reach using the same machinery developed in this paper.
